\section{Conclusion}
Telling a model what it cannot see is a cheap and inspectable alternative
to learning it. A few-millisecond CPU probe that writes camera motion in the
answers' own words, plus LoRA on an off-the-shelf video language model,
scores 3.5 points above a purpose-built motion architecture on ReVA, with
no new network parts and one GPU. Most of that margin comes from the newer
backbone and from fine-tuning on the seen videos; the fine-tuned model
reads the hint, but the hint itself adds little. Three findings outlast the benchmark: the test
regime is seen-video, so a development split without training-answer
leaks is essential; text
alone solves 56\%, so the video's true contribution must be measured against
a blind baseline; and a hint's wording matters more than the measurement
behind it. Temporal grounding remains the weakest task. Training with 32 shifted
frames and a drawn clock raised it by 2.7 points, but its errors are still
near-misses of about one timestamp. More broadly, we hope the paper
encourages reporting text-only baselines and per-task numbers in video QA
benchmarks, where they are still often left out.
